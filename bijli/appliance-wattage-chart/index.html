Wattage of common household appliances

Typical power ratings of household appliances, and the units each uses in an hour at that rating.

How to read the chart

Each row gives a typical rating for a common household model, the units it uses in one hour at that rating, and how long it takes to use one unit. Your own appliance may differ: the true figure is on its rating plate or star label. Appliances that switch on and off by themselves, such as fridges, air conditioners, geysers and irons, use less over a day than the rating multiplied by the hours.

Units in an hour are simply watts divided by 1,000. To turn the chart into your own monthly figure, use the units calculator, which starts from these values and lets you correct them.

What the chart shows at a glance

Heating and cooling dominate. Everything that makes heat (geyser, immersion rod, room heater, iron, kettle, induction cooktop, microwave oven) or moves heat (air conditioner) is rated in the thousands of watts. One hour of a room heater equals more than a week of an LED bulb left on day and night.

Hours matter as much as watts. A fridge and a router draw little but never stop. A ceiling fan running twelve hours a day through the summer uses more in a month than an iron, a mixer and a kettle together.

Lighting is no longer a big item, once bulbs and tubes are LED. A home lit entirely by LED uses only a few units a month on light.

Standby adds up quietly. Set-top boxes, televisions, chargers, microwave clocks and music systems draw a little all day. Individually trivial, together they may come to a few units a month.

Finding your own appliance's figure

  1. The rating plate: a sticker or stamp on the back, base or inside the door. It shows watts (W), or volts and amperes; watts are roughly volts × amperes.
  2. The star label, for air conditioners, refrigerators, fans and geysers, gives units per year under standard conditions. See star ratings.
  3. The manual or the maker's website, under specifications.
  4. A plug-in energy meter, which measures actual consumption over a day or a week. This is the only reliable way for a fridge or an old appliance.
  5. Your own electricity meter: note the reading, run only that appliance for an hour, and note it again. See the meter reading calculator.

Notes on particular appliances

  • Air conditioners: the rating is the draw at full load. Inverter models throttle down once the room is cool. See AC electricity use.
  • Refrigerators: the compressor runs only part of the time. The yearly units on the star label, divided by 365, give a daily figure.
  • Geysers: energy depends on the litres heated, not the hours switched on. See geyser use.
  • Ceiling fans: an energy-saving BLDC fan uses well under half the power of an ordinary fan at the same speed.
  • Water pumps: a motor's horsepower is its output; it draws more than that from the supply. An automatic level controller prevents wasted running.
  • Washing machines: most of the power goes to heating water where a hot wash is chosen; a cold wash uses far less.
  • Induction cooktops: the rating is the maximum; at a simmer they draw much less.
  • Inverters and UPS units: see inverter power use.

Load and wiring

Add the watts of the appliances that may run at the same time to estimate the load your connection must carry, in kW. Heavy appliances (above about 1,000 W) belong on 16 A sockets with proper earthing, one to a socket. See fixed charge and sanctioned load.

Which home appliance uses the most electricity?

By rating, room heaters, geysers, induction cooktops and air conditioners. By monthly units, usually the air conditioner in summer, the geyser in winter, and the refrigerator and fans all year.

How many watts is a ceiling fan?

An ordinary ceiling fan is about 70 to 80 watts at full speed; an energy-saving BLDC fan about 28 to 35 watts.

Are these wattages exact?

No. They are typical values. Check the rating plate or star label of your own appliance.
