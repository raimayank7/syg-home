Does a home inverter increase the electricity bill?

An inverter does add to the bill, a little when healthy and a lot when its batteries are failing. Here is where the units go.

How a home inverter works

A home inverter, often called a UPS, does two jobs. While mains power is present, it passes the supply through to the house and charges its battery. When the mains fail, it turns the battery's stored energy back into household current.

Neither step is perfect. Energy is lost as heat when the battery is charged, again when it is discharged, and in the electronics all the time.

Where the extra units come from

1. Round-trip loss. Of the electricity put into a lead-acid battery, only about three-quarters or less comes back out; the inverter's own conversion loses a little more. So every unit you use during a power cut costs noticeably more than one unit from the meter. Lithium batteries waste less.

2. Keeping the battery topped up. A battery slowly loses charge by itself, and the charger keeps replacing it, cut or no cut. A healthy system draws only a small trickle for this.

3. The inverter's own consumption. The electronics, display and fan draw some watts continuously.

For a healthy home system with few power cuts, all this comes to a modest number of units a month. In an area with long daily cuts, the units cycled through the battery, and so the losses, are larger.

When the inverter becomes a big consumer

An ageing or damaged battery can no longer hold charge. The charger then works at a high rate for hours every day, pouring in energy that turns to heat and gas. Signs:

  • the backup time has fallen sharply;
  • the battery is hot, smells of acid or rotten eggs, or bulges;
  • it needs distilled water very often;
  • the inverter shows charging all the time, or its fan never stops;
  • the meter advances steadily with everything else switched off.

A failing battery bank can add a surprising number of units a month, and it is a safety risk. Have it tested and replace it.

Keeping the loss small

  1. Size it sensibly. Put only the essential circuits on the inverter: some lights, fans, the router, a television. Do not connect the fridge, geyser, iron, air conditioner or pump unless the system is designed for them.
  2. Maintain lead-acid batteries: top up with distilled water to the mark, keep terminals clean and tight, and keep the battery in a ventilated place away from heat.
  3. Replace batteries at the end of their life, typically some years depending on type and use, instead of nursing a dead set.
  4. Choose efficient equipment when buying: a pure sine-wave inverter with good charging control, and a battery type suited to your pattern of power cuts.
  5. Switch the inverter off at its bypass when leaving the house closed for weeks, after a full charge, if the maker's instructions allow.
  6. Use efficient loads. LED lights and BLDC fans on the inverter circuit draw less from the battery, so less has to be put back.

Estimating your inverter's use

The simple way is to measure. With the mains on and the house's other loads switched off, read the meter over a few hours with the inverter connected, and again with it bypassed. The difference is its standing consumption. Or note what the bill was before and after a battery replacement.

In the units calculator, an entry for "Inverter or UPS charging" lets you include a typical figure; adjust it to what you measure.

Computer UPS units

The small UPS under a desktop computer works the same way on a small scale: a few watts continuously, more as its battery ages. If the computer is rarely used, switch the UPS off with it.

Inverter and solar

An inverter battery can be charged from rooftop solar panels with a suitable solar charge controller or hybrid inverter, which reduces what the mains must supply. That is a different arrangement from grid-connected rooftop solar with net metering. See rooftop solar basics.

Does an inverter increase the electricity bill?

Yes, somewhat. Charging losses and standby use add units every month, more where power cuts are long, and much more if the battery is failing.

How do I know if my inverter battery is wasting electricity?

Short backup, a hot or smelly battery, frequent water top-ups and a charger that runs constantly are the signs. Have the battery tested.
