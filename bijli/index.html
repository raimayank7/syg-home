Electricity bill calculator

Type the units you used and the rates on your bill. The page works out the bill slab by slab, with the fixed charge.

Energy charge slabs

How to use the calculator

  1. Choose the tariff. Pick "My own rates" and type the slabs from your bill, or pick the official Punjab (PSPCL) domestic tariff, which is built in.
  2. Type the units consumed in the bill period, and whether the bill covers one month or two.
  3. Type your sanctioned load in kW if your supplier charges a fixed amount per kW.
  4. Optionally add duty and other charges as a percentage, and any subsidy or rebate in rupees, to match your real bill.

The working appears at once: the units that fall in each slab, the rate, the amount, then the fixed charge and the total.

Why only one state's tariff is built in

Electricity tariffs are set separately for every state, and often for each distribution company within it, by that state's electricity regulatory commission. They are revised every year, sometimes in mid-year, and a bill also carries fuel adjustment, duty and subsidies that change more often still. A site that printed "current rates" for every state from memory or from other websites would be wrong somewhere at all times, and a wrong rate is worse than none.

So this site prints a tariff only where we have read the official order or the supplier's own document, and we show its date and source. At present that is Punjab (PSPCL), domestic supply for FY 2026-27; the details are on the Punjab tariff page. For every other supplier, the calculator works just as well with the rates you type from your own bill, which are by definition the right ones.

Entering your own rates

Look for the tariff or "rate schedule" on the back of the bill, or the lines of the bill that show units and rate. Then:

  • For each slab, type the upper limit in units and the rate per unit. A bill that reads "0 to 100 units at one rate, 101 to 300 at another, above 300 at a third" becomes three rows: 100, 300 and a last row with the limit left empty.
  • For a single flat rate, use one row with the limit empty.
  • Type the fixed charge, either per kW of sanctioned load or as a flat amount per month, as your bill shows it.
  • If the bill is for two months, choose two months: the slab limits and the fixed charge are doubled, which is how bimonthly bills are normally worked. If your supplier does it differently, enter the two-month limits yourself and choose one month.

Your rates are remembered in this browser for next time.

How slab billing works

Slabs are applied step by step, not to the whole consumption. If the first 100 units cost one rate and the next 200 another, a household using 250 units pays the first rate on 100 units and the second rate on 150 units. Crossing a slab limit by one unit raises the price of that one unit only. The guide to slab rates has worked examples. A few suppliers use a different method for some categories, where crossing a limit changes the rate for all units; your bill's own working will show which you have.

Why the result may differ from your bill

The calculator covers the energy charge and the fixed charge, plus what you add. A real bill can also include:

  • fuel and power purchase adjustment, a per-unit amount that changes from time to time;
  • electricity duty or tax levied by the state government;
  • meter rent, cesses and surcharges;
  • subsidy credited by the state government;
  • arrears, late payment surcharge, security deposit adjustments or interest on the deposit;
  • rounding.

Use the two extra boxes to bring the estimate into line. To understand each line of the bill, see how to read an electricity bill.

How do I calculate my electricity bill from units?

Multiply the units in each slab by that slab's rate, add the amounts, then add the fixed charge and any duty or other charges. The calculator shows this working line by line.

Is the result my exact bill?

It is an estimate of the energy and fixed charges from the rates entered. Duty, fuel adjustment, subsidy and arrears on your bill can change the final figure.
