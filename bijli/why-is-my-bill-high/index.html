Why is my electricity bill so high? A checklist

Before blaming the meter, read the bill line by line. Most "wrong" bills are explained by one of a dozen ordinary causes.

Step 1: is it the units, or the amount?

Compare this bill with the last one and with the same month last year.

  • Units are normal but the amount is high: go to step 2.
  • Units are high: go to step 3.

Step 2: the amount is high, the units are not

  1. Arrears. An earlier bill was unpaid, paid late, or paid after this bill was prepared. Check your payment receipts.
  2. Late payment surcharge.
  3. Additional security deposit, demanded after a review of your consumption.
  4. A tariff revision. New rates from the regulator's latest order, or a change in the fuel adjustment.
  5. A subsidy that stopped or was not applied, for example because consumption crossed the scheme's limit.
  6. Adjustment of earlier estimated bills.
  7. A changed tariff category or load, shown at the top of the bill.
  8. A longer bill period. Count the days between the reading dates: a bill for 40 days is naturally bigger than one for 30.

Each of these is printed as a separate line. See how to read your bill.

Step 3: the units are high

Check the reading first.

  • Is the present reading on the bill more than the meter shows today? Then it was misread or mistyped. Photograph the meter and complain at once.
  • Is the reading status "average", "provisional" or "estimated"? The units were guessed, and will be corrected at the next actual reading.
  • Does the previous reading match last bill's present reading? A gap means units billed twice or a missed bill now caught up. After several estimated low bills, one true reading collects the whole shortfall at once.
  • Is the meter number yours?

Then look at how you lived.

  • The season. The first hot month of air conditioning or the first cold month of geysers and heaters can double the units.
  • Guests, holidays at home, work from home, a new baby, exams.
  • A new appliance: an air conditioner, a second fridge, an induction cooktop, an electric vehicle charging at home.
  • Building work or a tenant's use on your meter.

Step 4: hunt for the hidden user

If neither the reading nor your habits explain it:

  1. Switch everything off at the main switch. The meter must stop. If it still advances, there is a wiring fault or a load you do not know of.
  2. Switch on one circuit at a time and watch the meter's blink rate or the kW display to find the heavy one.
  3. Suspects, in order: a geyser with a failed thermostat that never switches off; a water pump running because of a leaking tank or a stuck float switch; an old refrigerator with a bad door seal or failing compressor; an air conditioner low on gas, running constantly without cooling; an inverter with dying batteries; a stabiliser or UPS left on.
  4. Earth leakage: damp walls, damaged cables or a faulty appliance let current leak. An electrician tests this with a meter; an earth leakage breaker (RCCB or ELCB) that trips often is a warning.
  5. Someone else on your line: a shared staircase light, a neighbour's pump, or an old wire from a previous arrangement connected after your meter.

To find what one appliance uses, see the method on the meter reading page, or use a plug-in energy meter.

Step 5: if you believe the meter is wrong

Meters do fail, though less often than people think. Signs: the display is blank or frozen, the reading jumps, or it counts with the main switch off and no wiring fault found.

  • Apply to the supplier for a meter test. They test it, sometimes with a check meter fitted alongside for a period, for a fee set in their schedule that is usually returned if the meter is found faulty. The bill is revised under the supply code for the period the rule allows.
  • Do not open, shift or tamper with the meter yourself. That is an offence.

How to complain effectively

  1. Write through the supplier's app, portal or sub-division office: consumer number, bill number, what is wrong, and what you ask for.
  2. Attach a dated photograph of the meter and copies of the last few bills.
  3. Note the complaint number and the date.
  4. If it is not settled, go to the supplier's consumer grievance redressal forum, and then the electricity ombudsman. Their addresses are on the bill or the supplier's website. The process is free.
  5. Meanwhile, ask in writing how much you should pay while the dispute is examined, and pay that by the due date, so that surcharge and disconnection do not follow.

A warning

A message or call saying your power will be cut tonight because of an unpaid or "not updated" bill, with a mobile number to ring, is a fraud. See the electricity bill scam.

Can a faulty meter cause a high bill?

Yes, but reading errors, estimated bills, arrears and seasonal use are far commoner. Rule those out, then ask for a meter test.

My house was locked and the bill is still high. Why?

Check for an estimated reading, arrears and the fixed charge. Then test whether the meter advances with the main switch off: a fridge, pump, inverter or a wiring leak may have been running.
