Appliance units calculator: how many units will I use?

Add your appliances with their watts and daily hours. The page shows the units per day and per month, and which appliance uses the most.

The formula

Units (kWh) = watts × hours ÷ 1000

One unit of electricity is one kilowatt-hour: a 1,000-watt appliance running for one hour. So a 75-watt fan running 12 hours uses 75 × 12 ÷ 1000 = 0.9 units a day, and about 27 units in a 30-day month. The calculator does this for each appliance and adds them up.

How to use it

  1. Choose an appliance from the list. A typical wattage and typical daily hours are filled in.
  2. Correct the watts from the label on your own appliance if you can; see below. Correct the hours to your own use, and the number of such appliances.
  3. Tap Add. Repeat for everything that runs daily.
  4. Read the total. The table shows each appliance's share, and the result names the biggest user.

Type your rate per unit for a rough cost, or follow the link to the bill calculator for a slab-wise figure. The list is remembered in this browser.

Finding the real wattage

  • Look for the rating plate: a sticker or stamp on the back or underside, or inside the fridge door. It gives watts (W) or, for some appliances, amperes (A). Watts are roughly volts × amperes; at the usual 230 volts, 2 A is about 460 W.
  • The user manual or the maker's website lists it under specifications.
  • For air conditioners and refrigerators, the star label shows the units used in a year under standard test conditions, which is more useful than the watts. See star ratings.
  • A plug-in energy meter between the socket and the appliance measures real use over a day. It is the only way to know what an old fridge really draws.

Why real use differs from watts × hours

The rating plate shows the maximum draw. Many appliances do not run at that level continuously.

  • Refrigerators cycle on and off; the compressor may run a third to half of the time. The list assumes about ten running hours a day.
  • Air conditioners work hard until the room is cool and then ease off, more so inverter models. The setting, the room, the weather and insulation change the figure greatly.
  • Geysers and irons switch off at the set temperature.
  • Washing machines draw most when heating water, little when only tumbling.
  • Fans draw less at lower speeds, with electronic regulators.
  • Chargers, televisions and set-top boxes draw a little even on standby.

So treat the result as a fair estimate. If it is far below your bill, look at the appliances that run unseen: the water pump, the geyser left on, an old fridge, the inverter charging its batteries.

Reading the result

The few appliances that either draw a lot (air conditioner, geyser, heater, induction, iron) or run all day (fridge, fans, router) make up most of any bill. Lights and phone chargers matter little once the bulbs are LED. The result's "biggest user" line tells you where a change in habit will show on the bill. The guide to reducing the bill starts there.

From units to a bill

Units are only half of the bill. Slab rates mean that the last units cost more than the first, and a fixed charge is added whatever you use. Carry the monthly units into the bill calculator with your own rates.

How many units does a 1.5 ton AC use?

It depends on the model's efficiency, the setting and the hours. As a rough guide, an inverter model drawing about 1,500 watts at full load uses 1 to 1.5 units an hour while cooling hard and less once the room is cool. The star label gives the yearly figure for your model.

How many units does a fan use in a day?

An ordinary 75-watt ceiling fan uses about 0.9 units in 12 hours; an energy-saving BLDC fan of about 30 watts uses about 0.36.

What is 1 unit of electricity?

One kilowatt-hour: 1,000 watts used for one hour, or 100 watts for ten hours.
