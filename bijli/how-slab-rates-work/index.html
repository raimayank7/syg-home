How slab rates work on an electricity bill

Most home tariffs charge a low rate for the first block of units and higher rates for later blocks. Each block is charged at its own rate.

The idea

Regulators set home tariffs in slabs so that a basic amount of electricity is cheap and heavy use costs more. A tariff might read, for illustration only:

  • first 100 units at rate A,
  • next 200 units (101 to 300) at rate B,
  • everything above 300 at rate C,

with A lower than B and B lower than C. These letters stand in for rupee figures; your own bill gives the real ones for your supplier.

The usual method: each block at its own rate

Under the normal method, often called telescopic billing, units are filled into the slabs in order, like water filling a row of tanks. A household that uses 350 units pays:

  • 100 units × A,
  • 200 units × B,
  • 50 units × C.

It does not pay rate C on all 350 units. The higher rate applies only to the units that fall in the higher slab.

A worked example with real official figures

Take the Punjab domestic tariff for FY 2026-27 for a connection of up to 2 kW, from the official order: ₹3.85 per unit for the first 300 units and ₹7.05 per unit above 300. (Source and date are on the Punjab tariff page.)

What crossing a slab really costs

People often fear that using "one unit too many" raises the whole bill. Under the telescopic method it does not. Going from 300 to 301 units in the example adds exactly one unit at the higher rate: ₹7.05. What does change is the cost of every further unit: once you are in the higher slab, each extra hour of the air conditioner is charged at the higher rate. That is why savings at the top of your consumption are worth the most.

The exception: when the whole bill changes

Some tariffs, for some categories, are non-telescopic: if total consumption crosses a limit, a different rate table applies to all units, not only those above the limit. Under such a rule, crossing the limit by a few units does raise the whole bill noticeably. Some states also tie a subsidy or a free allowance to staying within a limit, which has the same effect. Your bill's own working shows which method applies: check whether the first units are still charged at the lowest rate when your consumption is high.

One-month and two-month bills

If your supplier bills every two months, the slab limits are normally doubled for the two-month bill, so that you are not pushed into higher slabs merely because the period is longer. Likewise, when a bill covers more or fewer days than usual, suppliers adjust the slabs in proportion to the days. The bill shows the limits used.

The fixed charge is separate

Slabs apply to the energy charge only. The fixed charge, based on your sanctioned load, is added whatever you consume. See fixed charge and sanctioned load.

Using slabs to cut the bill

  • Find which slab your usual consumption ends in, from past bills.
  • The saving from using less is worth the rate of that last slab, not the average rate.
  • If you are just inside a higher slab, modest changes (the geyser timer, the air conditioner setting, replacing an old fan) can bring the top units down at the highest rate you pay. See how to reduce the bill.
If I cross a slab, do all my units cost more?

Under the usual telescopic method, no. Only the units above the limit are charged at the higher rate. A few tariffs are non-telescopic, where crossing a limit changes the rate for all units; your bill shows which applies.

What does "telescopic tariff" mean?

Each block of units is charged at its own rate, so the bill is the sum of the blocks.
