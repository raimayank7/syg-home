Star ratings on appliances: how to read the energy label

The stars are a quick guide. The number that matters for your bill is the units per year printed on the same label.

What the label is

In India, the Bureau of Energy Efficiency (BEE), a body under the Ministry of Power, runs the standards and labelling programme. Appliances covered by it carry a label with one to five stars: more stars mean the model uses less electricity for the same job than models with fewer stars, under a standard test. For some appliances the label is compulsory, for others voluntary; the list is on BEE's official website.

What is printed on it

A typical label shows:

  • the stars, out of five;
  • the electricity consumption in units per year (kWh/year), or for fans and some others a service-value figure;
  • the appliance type, brand and model, and capacity (tonnage, litres, sweep size);
  • for air conditioners, the efficiency ratio (ISEER), where a higher number is better;
  • the label period: the years for which that star table is valid.

Read the units, not only the stars

Two models with the same stars can differ in yearly units, and capacity matters: a five-star 2-ton air conditioner uses more than a three-star 1-ton one, because it does more work. So compare:

  1. models of the same capacity;
  2. their units per year;
  3. then the price.

What a difference in units is worth

Yearly saving = difference in units per year × your rate per unit

If one refrigerator's label says 200 units a year and another's says 300, the first saves 100 units a year. Multiply by the rate of the slab your consumption usually ends in (see slab rates), then by the years you expect to keep it, and compare with the extra price. For appliances that run all day or all summer, a higher rating usually repays itself; for something used a few minutes a day, it may not.

The label year matters

BEE tightens the star tables from time to time. A model that earned five stars under an older table may earn only three or four under the current one. When comparing, look at the label period printed on each label, and prefer the units-per-year figures, which do not depend on the star table. A "five-star" appliance bought many years ago is not five-star by today's standard.

The test and real life

The yearly units figure comes from a standard test: a set number of running hours, at set temperatures. Your use will differ. An air conditioner run twelve hours a day in a top-floor room uses far more than its label; one run two hours a day uses far less. The label is for comparing models, not for predicting your bill. Estimate your own use with the units calculator.

By appliance

  • Air conditioners: compare ISEER and yearly units for the same tonnage. Inverter models generally rate higher. See AC electricity use.
  • Refrigerators: running all day, every day, they repay a good rating over their long life. Direct-cool and frost-free types have separate tables.
  • Ceiling fans: energy-efficient BLDC models use well under half the power of ordinary ones. Fans run for many hours, so the difference is large over a year.
  • Geysers: the rating reflects how well the tank holds heat (standing loss). It matters most if the geyser is left on for long. See geyser use.
  • Televisions, washing machines, lamps, pumps and others have their own labels.

Checking a label

Makers register their labelled models with BEE, and BEE publishes search tools and a mobile app for checking a model's rating and consumption. If a label looks odd, shows no validity period, or the model cannot be found in BEE's records, be cautious.

Beyond the label

A well-rated appliance still wastes power if it is the wrong size or badly used. Size the air conditioner to the room and the fridge to the family, install them properly, and maintain them. See how to reduce the bill.

Is a 5-star appliance always worth the extra price?

For appliances that run long hours, such as fridges, fans and air conditioners in hot regions, usually yes. Work out the yearly saving from the units on the labels and your rate per unit.

Why does my old 5-star AC use more than a new 3-star one?

The star tables are tightened every few years, and efficiency has improved. Compare the units per year on the labels.

Who issues the star rating?

The Bureau of Energy Efficiency, under the Ministry of Power.
