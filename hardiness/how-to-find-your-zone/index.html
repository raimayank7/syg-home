How to find and use your hardiness zone

Looking up the zone takes seconds. Using it well takes a few more habits.

Finding your zone

  1. By ZIP code. Type your five-digit ZIP code on the home page of this site. The answer comes from the USDA's ZIP code table, which lists the zone of each ZIP code area.
  2. On the official map. The USDA site offers an interactive map where you can type a ZIP code or click on your location, and downloadable state maps. The map is drawn in squares about half a mile on a side, which is finer than older versions.
  3. A ZIP code covers an area. Large rural ZIP codes can contain more than one zone. If you live near a boundary, or on a hill or in a valley, check the official map at your own location.

Using it well

The USDA advises treating the map as a general guide for perennials, and gives these cautions, which we summarise.

  • Choose plants rated for your zone or colder. A plant listed for zone 5 to 9 is a safe choice in zone 7.
  • Be careful at the edge. A plant that is only just hardy in your zone may thrive for years and then be lost in a rare cold snap.
  • Remember other factors. Wind, soil type, soil moisture, humidity, snow cover, winter sun and how and where the plant was planted all affect survival. Warm-season heat and moisture matter too.
  • Ask local experts. The USDA recommends consulting established nurseries, Master Gardeners or the local extension service for conditions in your area.
  • Do not panic if your zone changed. The USDA says that a change of zone in the new edition does not mean you should remove plants or change what you grow; what has thrived will most likely continue to thrive.

Places without a ZIP code

The table covers ZIP codes. If you are in a rural area with no useful ZIP code, or outside the US, use the official map where you can click a point, or the lowest winter temperatures in local weather records, and match them to the temperature ranges in the zone table on What is a hardiness zone.

A habit worth building

Keep a note of the coldest night each winter in your garden. After a few years you will know how your own garden compares with the zone average, which is more useful than any map.

Source: USDA Plant Hardiness Zone Map, "How to Use the Maps" (planthardiness.ars.usda.gov), read on 13 November 2026.

Which zone do I use if I am between two?

Use the colder one when choosing plants, because a cold snap affects the whole garden.

Does my zone tell me when to plant?

No. Planting dates depend on frost dates and soil temperature. See zones are not frost dates.
