Reading plant tags and catalogues for zones

A plant label gives a range of zones. Here is how to read it and what it does not promise.

What the USDA says about zones and plant descriptions

The USDA notes that zone numbers are typically listed with the descriptions of perennial plants in catalogues and other sales information from nurseries and plant suppliers, and that the zones can help in choosing perennial plants based on how they have performed in other regions of the country. A description that gives a zone is therefore a statement about cold: the map is built only from the average annual extreme minimum winter temperature. If the zone from the ZIP code lookup is the zone, or a warmer zone, than the one given for the plant, the winter lows of your area are, on average, no colder than the plant is described for. The map says nothing about summer heat, so a plant described for a range of zones may also have a warm-side limit that this map does not measure: check the grower's description.

An annual completes its life in one season, so it does not need a hardiness zone, only a safe planting date. See zones are not frost dates.

Pushing the zone

Gardeners sometimes grow plants rated for a warmer zone than their own, usually in a sheltered microclimate. The USDA cautions that growing plants at the extreme edge of their zone range means a rare cold snap lasting a day or two can kill plants that thrived for years. If you try, put them in the warmest, most sheltered place and expect the occasional loss.

Other limits not on the tag

Moisture, soil, light, wind, humidity and the plant's size and health when planted also determine survival, according to the USDA. A plant that is hardy on paper can fail in a soggy winter soil or an exposed windy site.

Before you buy

Check your zone on the home page, read the tag's range, think about the planting spot, and ask a local nursery whether the plant has done well locally. Local experience beats general charts.

Source: USDA Plant Hardiness Zone Map, "How to Use the Maps" (planthardiness.ars.usda.gov), read on 13 November 2026.

What if a plant description gives no zone?

Ask the seller for the cold hardiness of the plant, or ask a local nursery or extension service.

Is a zone 7 plant safe in zone 6?

The USDA warns that plants at the edge of their cold range can be lost in a rare cold snap. A zone 6 winter is colder on average than a zone 7 winter, so ask local growers.
