Paint calculator

Type the room size, the doors and windows and the number of coats. You get litres, US gallons and a sensible set of tins to buy.

How the answer is worked out

The calculator finds the wall area first: the distance round the room (twice the length plus the width) multiplied by the wall height. A door is taken off as 1.8 square metres (2.0 m by 0.9 m, about 19 sq ft) and a window as 1.44 square metres (1.2 m by 1.2 m, about 15.5 sq ft). Those are typical sizes, so a very large window or a double door will make the real figure a little smaller than the one shown. The ceiling is added only if you tick the box.

The paintable area is then multiplied by the number of coats and divided by the coverage of the paint, in square metres per litre. Emulsion tins usually print a range of coverage on the label, often somewhere between 9 and 13 square metres a litre for one coat on a smooth, primed wall. We start at 10, which is a cautious figure, and you can change it to whatever your tin says. Rough plaster, dark old colours and bare new plaster all soak up more paint, so use a lower figure for them.

Finally 10 per cent is added for cutting in along edges, the paint left in the roller tray and tin and a little touching up later. The last step picks tin sizes. Paint in India is commonly sold in 1, 4, 10 and 20 litre tins, so the page looks for the combination that gets you at least the amount you need with the least left over.

What is formula and what is assumption

The formula is simple and you can check it by hand: paintable area x coats / coverage x (1 + extra). No official coverage figure applies to every paint (the tin states the coverage of that paint), so the starting values here are assumptions that you can change: 10 square metres per litre per coat, 10 per cent extra, a door of 1.8 m2 and a window of 1.44 m2. Replace them with your own.

Things the calculator cannot know

It does not know how thirsty your walls are. New plaster takes a coat of primer or a thinned first coat before you start counting. A big colour change, such as dark red to white, can need three coats however the arithmetic comes out. Textured walls, brick and rough concrete use much more paint than smooth plaster. Spray painting wastes more than a roller. And colours mixed at the shop are made for one batch: if you will need more later, buy a little extra now, because a second batch mixed from the same code can look slightly different on the wall.

Always read the tin. Its coverage and drying times beat any general figure, including ours.

Does the calculator include the skirting, doors and window frames?

No. It counts only the wall and ceiling surface. Woodwork is normally painted with a different paint and a small amount, so work that out separately: one litre of gloss or enamel covers a few doors or a run of skirting.

How much paint do I need for one coat?

Set the coats box to 1. The litres are simply halved compared with two coats. Most walls need two coats, because the first coat rarely hides the old colour or gives an even finish.

Why does it add 10 per cent?

Some paint is always left on the roller, the tray and the tin walls, and cutting in at corners and edges is wasteful. Ten per cent is a common allowance for an ordinary room; use 15 or 20 for rough walls or a sprayer.

Can I use feet instead of metres?

Yes. Choose feet or metres in the unit box and the page does the conversion. The answer is given in litres and in US gallons.

Estimates only. Real jobs differ with the surface, the product and the skill of the worker. Read the maker's instructions on the tin, bag or box, and order a little extra.
