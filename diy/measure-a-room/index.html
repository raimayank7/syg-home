How to measure a room for paint, tiles and flooring

Most wrong orders come from a bad measurement, not from bad arithmetic. This is the method that avoids the usual mistakes.

What you need

A steel tape of at least five metres (16 ft), a pencil, paper and a phone for photographs. A cloth tape stretches and a sagging tape reads long over a big room, so use a rigid steel one and have someone hold the far end if the room is longer than the tape. A laser measure is quicker, but check it against the tape once.

Step by step

  1. Draw the room as seen from above, however roughly. Mark the doors, windows, alcoves, chimney breasts and cupboards.
  2. Measure each wall along the floor, not at head height. Skirting boards, plaster thickness and bulging walls can make the measure at the top differ by a centimetre or two.
  3. Measure the length and the width in two places each, near each end. If they differ by more than a centimetre or two, the room is not square; use the larger number for buying, and note the difference for the layout.
  4. Measure the ceiling height at a corner and the middle. Old houses and sloping ceilings vary.
  5. Measure every opening: height and width of each door and window. Write the sizes next to the drawing.
  6. For an L-shaped or oddly-shaped room, split it into rectangles, measure each and add the areas.
  7. Write units beside every number. Mixing feet and metres on the same sketch is a classic reason for ordering half what is needed.

What to count, and what to leave out

For paint, you need the wall area (perimeter x height) and, if painting it, the ceiling area (length x width). Take out doors and windows if large. For tiles and flooring, you need the area of the floor you actually cover. Include the bit under a door and any alcove, and leave out the footprint of fitted units that will never move. For wallpaper you need the total length of wall and the height, not the area.

Common mistakes

  • Measuring the room in the old plan rather than the finished room, after the plaster has changed the size.
  • Forgetting to convert inches to feet (7 ft 6 in is 7.5 ft, not 7.6).
  • Measuring a wall in a room with a bay window straight across the bay and missing the extra floor in the bay.
  • Using the advertised flat area from a brochure. Carpet area is the floor you can actually use; see areas and units.
  • Not measuring twice. A second measure, by another person if possible, takes five minutes and saves a return trip.

Take photographs of the room with the tape across the main dimensions; they help when you call a shop later to check a figure.

Should I measure in feet or metres?

Whichever you read most easily, and use only one unit on the sketch. The calculators on this site accept either.

What if the room is not rectangular?

Split it into rectangles and triangles, or measure each wall and enter the room as the largest rectangle plus separate pieces.

Estimates only. Real jobs differ with the surface, the product and the skill of the worker. Read the maker's instructions on the tin, bag or box, and order a little extra.
