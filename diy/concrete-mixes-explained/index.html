Concrete mixes explained

A mix like 1:2:4 is a recipe by volume. Understanding it makes the calculator results make sense.

Three numbers, three ingredients

A ratio of 1:2:4 means one part cement, two parts sand and four parts coarse aggregate (stone chips or gravel), measured by *volume* and not weight. The parts can be any container: a bucket, a box or a basket, as long as you use the same one for all three. Water is added last and is not part of the ratio.

The richer the mix (more cement for the same volume), the stronger the concrete, and the more it costs. A ratio of 1:1.5:3 has more cement per cubic metre than 1:2:4, which has more than 1:3:6.

The grade names

Concrete is also described by a grade: the "M" stands for mix and the number is the characteristic compressive strength in newtons per square millimetre, tested at 28 days. The Indian code IS 456:2000 (Table 9, read on 13 November 2026) gives nominal mixes for the lower grades by *mass*:

The code says the fine to coarse proportion is generally 1:2 but within 1:1.5 and 1:2.5, adjusted as the sand gets finer and the largest stone gets larger. The ratios many people quote by volume, such as 1:3:6, 1:2:4 and 1:1.5:3, are traditional site ratios. They are not in this table, so do not treat them as an exact equivalent of M10, M15 or M20. For important structures the engineer specifies a *design mix* with measured weights, and the site must follow it. This site gives quantity estimates and never replaces the specification.

Water

Water is the part of the recipe people get wrong. More water makes the mix easy to pour, and it also makes the concrete weaker and more likely to crack. The aim is a mix that is workable but not soupy. A rough guide for small jobs is to add water gradually until the mix just holds its shape when squeezed, and to avoid pouring a slurry. Clean water only: no sea water, no dirty pond water.

Curing

After pouring, concrete gains strength by reacting with water, not by drying. Keep it damp by covering it with wet sacking or plastic, or by sprinkling it with water for at least a week. A slab left to dry fast in the sun will crack and stay weak on its surface.

Mixing

Hand mixing: spread the sand on a clean hard floor, add the cement, mix the dry materials until the colour is even, add the aggregate, mix again, make a hollow and add water in stages. A drum mixer is faster and gives a more uniform result. Measure with the same bucket for each ingredient; dimensions of "a shovel" differ.

From the mix to the calculator

The concrete calculator takes the volume you need, multiplies by an assumed 1.54 for the dry materials (a box you can change) and splits it by the ratio. See also cement, sand and aggregate units for how those materials are bought.

Is 1:2:4 the same as M15?

Not exactly. IS 456 gives its M15 nominal mix by mass (330 kg of dry aggregate per 50 kg of cement), not as 1:2:4 by volume, and the grade itself is a strength requirement, not a recipe.

Can I add extra sand to get more volume?

You will get a weaker concrete with more cracks. Keep to the ratio.

How long before I can walk on a slab?

Typically a day or two for light foot traffic, longer in cold weather; ask your builder for heavy loads.

Estimates only. Real jobs differ with the surface, the product and the skill of the worker. Read the maker's instructions on the tin, bag or box, and order a little extra. Structural work (columns, beams, roof slabs, retaining walls) must be designed by a qualified engineer. This is an independent site.
