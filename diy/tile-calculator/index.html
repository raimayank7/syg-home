Tile calculator

Give the area, the size of one tile and how much waste to allow. You get the number of tiles and, if you know the box size, the boxes.

What the calculator does

It divides the area to be tiled by the area one tile takes up *including its joint*. A 600 mm square tile with a 3 mm joint covers a cell of 603 mm by 603 mm, which is 0.3636 square metres, not 0.36. Ignoring the joint makes little difference on a small wall, but over a big floor it adds up to one or two tiles missing.

Next comes the waste allowance. Tiles are cut at the edges of the room, round pipes and at doorways, and some break or arrive chipped. Ten per cent is the usual allowance for a straight layout in a plain rectangular room. Use 15 per cent for diagonal laying, for a room with many corners and alcoves, or for large tiles that are harder to use up in cuts. The total is rounded up to whole tiles, and to whole boxes if you tell it how many tiles a box holds.

A worked example

A kitchen floor of 12 square metres (about 129 sq ft), laid with 600 mm tiles, a 3 mm joint, 10 per cent waste, four tiles to a box.

  • One cell is 0.603 x 0.603 = 0.3636 m2.
  • 12 / 0.3636 = 33.0 tiles with no waste.
  • 33.0 x 1.10 = 36.3, rounded up to 37 tiles.
  • 37 tiles in boxes of four is 9.25, so 10 boxes, which is 40 tiles. You get three spare.

The same 12 square metres in 300 mm tiles with the same joint needs 144 tiles, because the smaller tiles have more joints and more waste cuts.

What is formula and what is assumption

The formula is area / (tile plus joint area) x (1 + waste), rounded up, and you can check it by hand. The waste percentage, the joint width and the tile thickness are assumptions you set: no official figure applies to every room, because the right allowance depends on the layout and the person laying. Our starting values (10 per cent, 3 mm, 9 mm) are common choices, not rules.

Grout and adhesive

The page also shows the volume of the joints: the space between the tiles, as deep as the tile is thick (9 mm unless you change it). It is a solid volume, not a bag count. Powder grout and tile adhesive state their own coverage on the bag, and that depends on the joint width, the tile thickness and the trowel notch for adhesive. Use the bag's figure with your area. We do not print adhesive weights here, because a number from a table would be wrong for half the products on sale.

Buying advice that saves money

Buy all tiles for one room in one go and check that the batch (shade) code on every box is the same. Tiles from different batches can differ in colour and in size by a millimetre or two, which shows in a straight run. Keep two or three spare tiles for repairs: a dropped pan can chip a tile years later, and the same pattern is often discontinued. When you work out the area, include only the surfaces to be tiled and subtract fixed bath panels and the footprint of a kitchen island if the tiles will not go under it. Read how much tile waste to allow for the reasoning behind the percentages.

Do I need to add waste if I am buying exactly the tiles for a small splashback?

Yes, but a splashback is small, so even 15 per cent may be only two or three tiles. Rounding up usually covers it.

What box size should I enter?

Read it on the box or in the shop's listing: it states tiles per box and square metres per box. If you only know square metres per box, divide that by the area of one tile.

Is the area in the calculator the floor area or the tile area?

It is the floor or wall area to be covered. The tile size is entered separately.

Can I use inches?

Yes. Choose inches, centimetres or millimetres for the tile size, and feet or metres for the room.
