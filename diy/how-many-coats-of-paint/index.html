How many coats of paint do you need?

Two coats is the standard answer, but there are cases where it is one, three, or one coat of primer plus two.

The usual rule

Two coats of finish paint is the standard for walls. The first coat gets the colour onto the wall and is often uneven where it dries; the second coat builds a full, even colour and gives the paint its designed sheen and washability. "One-coat" paints are made thicker and can sometimes finish in a single coat over a similar, sound colour, but even then two coats look more even and wear longer.

When it is one coat

  • Repainting the same colour on a clean wall in good condition.
  • Freshening a light colour over a light colour.
  • Touching up a small area. Paint the whole wall corner to corner, though, so the new paint does not look like a patch.

When it is three coats, or primer and two

  • New plaster or bare cement. A primer or sealer first (or a thinned first coat if the maker says so), then two coats of finish.
  • Dark to light. Painting white over deep red or navy can need three coats. A tinted primer helps: tint the primer toward the new colour.
  • Stains. Water marks, smoke, crayon and grease bleed through ordinary paint. Seal them with a stain-blocking primer first.
  • Glossy surfaces. Sand lightly to give the paint something to grip and use a bonding primer.
  • Bright reds, yellows and some deep blues. These pigments are translucent and often need three coats whatever the tin says.

Ceilings and woodwork

Ceilings are usually two coats of ceiling paint; one coat shows lap marks. Woodwork and metal are painted with primer, then undercoat if the maker asks for it, then one or two topcoats of enamel or gloss. Always let each coat dry fully: the tin states the time between coats. Painting over a coat that is not dry pulls the layer below off the wall.

What it means for the quantity

The calculator multiplies the area by the number of coats, so going from two coats to three adds half again. The primer is a different product and has its own coverage, usually somewhat better than a finish paint, so work it out separately with one coat. For a bedroom of 50 m2 of paintable area at 10 m2/L: 10 litres for two coats and 15 for three. The paint calculator lets you set the number of coats and see the change.

A sensible habit

Buy for the coats you expect plus a small reserve. If the first coat looks much better than expected, you have paint left for a touch-up in a year. If it looks worse than expected, you have it on hand and do not need to match a batch again.

Can I use a primer-and-paint-in-one?

They are thicker and can reduce the number of steps, but they do not make a bad surface good. For bare plaster or stains still use a proper primer.

Does the paint finish change the coats?

Matt hides small flaws better. Gloss and satin show them, so the surface has to be smoother, but the number of coats is the same.

Estimates only. Real jobs differ with the surface, the product and the skill of the worker. Read the maker's instructions on the tin, bag or box, and order a little extra. Structural work (columns, beams, roof slabs, retaining walls) must be designed by a qualified engineer. This is an independent site.
