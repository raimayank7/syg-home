Concrete calculator

Enter the length, width and thickness. You get the volume of concrete, then the bags of cement, sand and coarse aggregate for the mix you choose.

From a slab to a shopping list

The first step is the plain geometry: length x width x thickness gives the volume of concrete in cubic metres. A 5 m by 4 m slab 100 mm (0.1 m) thick is 2 cubic metres. Five per cent is added for spillage, uneven ground and the slightly deeper edges you always end up with, which makes 2.1 cubic metres. That is the wet volume you would order from a ready-mix plant, where it is also sold in cubic yards (2.75 yd3 here).

If you will mix on site, the materials you must buy are more than the wet volume, because dry cement, sand and stone fill each other's gaps and shrink when mixed with water. The usual rule is that the dry volume of materials is about 1.54 times the wet volume. The dry volume is then divided between cement, sand and aggregate in the ratio of the mix. In a 1:2:4 mix there are 7 parts: one of cement, two of sand and four of stone.

A 50 kg bag of cement is taken as 0.0347 cubic metres, which is 50 kg at an assumed bulk density of 1,440 kg per cubic metre (an estimating assumption, editable in the calculator).

Choosing the mix

The three ratios offered (1:3:6, 1:2:4 and 1:1.5:3) are traditional volume ratios of cement to sand to coarse aggregate that builders use. They are not a table of the Indian concrete code. IS 456:2000, Table 9 (read on 13 November 2026), gives nominal mixes for grades M5 to M20 by mass: the total dry aggregate per 50 kg of cement is 800, 625, 480, 330 and 250 kg for M5, M7.5, M10, M15 and M20, the fine to coarse aggregate proportion is generally 1:2 (not more than 1:1.5 and not less than 1:2.5), and water is at most 60, 45, 34, 32 and 30 litres per 50 kg of cement. Which mix a job needs is for the engineer's specification: columns, beams and roof slabs must be designed. This page only counts materials for the volume ratio you choose, using the assumptions listed below, which you can change.

Assumptions you can change

The dry volume factor (1.54: dry materials needed per unit of wet concrete) and the cement bulk density (1,440 kg per cubic metre, which makes a 50 kg bag 0.0347 m3) are common estimating assumptions, not figures from a standard. They are boxes in the calculator: change them if your engineer or supplier uses other values. The waste percentage is also yours to set.

Accuracy and waste

Real jobs vary. Sand that is damp takes up more volume than dry sand (bulking), the sub-base may sink, and formwork may bulge. The page lets you change the waste percentage: 5 per cent for a flat slab on firm ground, 10 for footings in trenches that were dug by hand. A slab with a fine finished surface may also be given a screed on top; use the plaster and screed calculator for that layer.

Units

Sand and aggregate are sold in different units in different places: cubic metres, cubic feet, tonnes, or in parts of India by the brass (100 cubic feet). The result lists cubic metres and cubic feet so that you can ask your supplier in the unit they use. See cement, sand and aggregate units.

How many bags of cement make a cubic metre of concrete?

Per cubic metre of wet concrete you need about 6.3 bags for a 1:2:4 mix (1.54 / 7 = 0.22 m3 of cement) and about 8 bags for a 1:1.5:3 mix. The calculator works the exact figure for your volume.

Is the thickness in millimetres or metres?

You choose: millimetres, centimetres, inches, feet or metres in the thickness box.

Does it include reinforcement steel?

No. Steel is specified by the designer of the slab or beam.

Why is the dry volume 1.54 times the wet volume?

Mixing and compacting squeezes the air out of the loose dry materials. 1.54 is an estimating assumption, not a figure from a standard; it is editable in the calculator, and some estimators use slightly different values.

Estimates only. Real jobs differ with the surface, the product and the skill of the worker. Read the maker's instructions on the tin, bag or box, and order a little extra. Structural work (columns, beams, roof slabs, retaining walls) must be designed by a qualified engineer. This is an independent site.
