Where the weights come from: the USDA data

Every weight on this site is a published figure. This page explains which one and how reliable it is.

The source

The weights come from USDA FoodData Central, the food composition database of the United States Department of Agriculture. It is published as open data in the public domain. We used the SR Legacy release (April 2018), the final version of the long-running National Nutrient Database for Standard Reference. The file we read, food_portion.csv, lists for each food the weight in grams of common household measures: a cup, a tablespoon, a teaspoon, one egg, a slice and so on. We downloaded it in November 2026 and picked 124 ingredients commonly used in Indian and general home cooking.

What a household measure is

When the USDA analysed a food, it also recorded how much a familiar portion weighed. A "cup, chopped" of onions means the USDA weighed a US cup filled with chopped onion and recorded the weight (160 g). The figures are averages from a small number of weighings or from the food industry. They are intended for nutrition calculations and work very well for the kitchen, but they are not guaranteed for your particular flour or onions.

How we use it

  • The US cup weight on each page is taken from the first cup record for that food. Where the USDA gives several (chopped, sliced, packed) we list them all in a table.
  • Spoon weights come from the tablespoon and teaspoon records when they exist.
  • If the USDA gives only a spoon (most spices), the cup figure is worked out: 16 tablespoons or 48 teaspoons to a US cup. The page says so.
  • The metric cup column converts the US cup weight to a 250 ml cup by multiplying by 250 / 236.588.
  • Ounces use 28.3495 g, and tablespoons, teaspoons and cups in the "grams to cups" tables are simple division.

We round to whole grams for cups and to one decimal for spoons. Nothing is changed or adjusted from the data.

Names

The USDA names are American. Maida appears as "wheat flour, white, all-purpose", atta as "wheat flour, whole-grain", sooji as "semolina", besan as "chickpea flour", jeera as "cumin seed". Ghee has no record of its own, so we use "butter oil, anhydrous", which is made the same way (butter heated until the water and milk solids are removed). Each page shows the USDA name it used, and this matters when you compare, because Indian varieties differ: Indian wheat is often harder than the US soft wheat, basmati is not a USDA long-grain rice, and chakki atta is coarser than the standard whole-wheat flour.

What the data cannot tell you

  • Brand-to-brand variation, grind, moisture and storage.
  • Packing: the same ingredient weighs more in a pressed cup than in a loose one (for brown sugar the USDA gives 220 g packed and 145 g unpacked).
  • Indian-only ingredients, such as paneer, jaggery and many regional dals, that the USDA does not list.
  • Anything about nutrition or health: this site is only about weights.

If you find a figure that looks wrong for your ingredient, trust your scale. Weigh a level cup once and keep your own number.

Is the data current?

SR Legacy was frozen in April 2018. Household measures of flour and sugar do not change, so it remains a good reference.

Can I use the data for commercial purposes?

USDA data is in the public domain, but this site's text and layout are ours.
