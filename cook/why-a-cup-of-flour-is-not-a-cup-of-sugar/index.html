Why a cup of flour is not a cup of sugar

A cup is a volume. The weight inside it depends on the ingredient and how you fill it.

Space, not weight

A measuring cup holds a fixed amount of space: 236.6 millilitres for a US cup, 250 millilitres for the metric cup used in most other countries. Fill it with water and it holds about 237 grams. Fill it with honey and it holds about 339 grams, because honey packs more matter into the same space. Fill it with rolled oats and it holds about 81 grams, because oats are flakes with air between them. Same cup, very different weights.

This is why a recipe that says "1 cup" is only exact if you also know the ingredient and how it was measured. The converter on this site covers that: choose the ingredient and it uses the weight of that ingredient's cup.

Why the same ingredient varies

For a powder or a grain, the weight in the cup depends on how closely it is packed.

  • Scooped. If you push the cup into the flour bin, you compress the flour, and the cup holds more flour than a spooned cup does. The USDA records one weight per cup, so a scooped cup of flour will normally exceed it.
  • Spooned and levelled. If you spoon flour lightly into the cup and sweep the top level with a knife, you get the lighter figure that most recipes assume.
  • Sifted. Sifting adds air. A cup of sifted icing sugar weighs noticeably less than a cup that was not sifted: USDA records 120 g unsifted and 100 g sifted.
  • Packed. Brown sugar is meant to be pressed in until the cup holds its shape, and the packed cup weighs far more than a loose one (220 g against 145 g in the USDA record).

For chopped and cut ingredients the shape matters too: a cup of chopped walnuts weighs more than a cup of ground walnuts, because grinding breaks the pieces into powder that fills the air gaps differently, and a cup of sliced carrot weighs more than a cup of grated carrot.

Water as a yardstick

A handy check is how an ingredient compares with water. Fats and oils are lighter than water per cup. Flours are about half. Sugar is a bit under. Syrups, honey and condensed milk are well above. Spices and herbs vary greatly. The ingredient pages here show each item as a percentage of the weight of a cup of water so you can see where it sits.

What to do about it

For everyday dishes such as dal or sabzi the difference barely matters. For baking, where flour, sugar and fat must be balanced, small errors change the result: a cake made with a scooped cup of flour is dry and dense. Weighing the flour in grams is the cure. If the recipe gives only cups, use the figures here and spoon and level your flour.

Is a cup of flour always 125 grams?

No. The USDA gives about 125 g for white flour and 120 g for wholewheat, but a scooped cup can weigh more. Use 125 g as the baseline for a spooned and levelled cup.

Why do American recipes use cups?

They are quick and need no scale. The cost is the variation described above.
