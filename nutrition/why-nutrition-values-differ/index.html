Why nutrition values differ between sources

Two reliable sources can give different numbers for one food. This guide explains the common reasons.

Food is not a constant

A food database gives one number for "apple" or "lentils". Real foods vary: a sweet ripe mango and a hard unripe one, a wheat grown in dry soil and one grown in rich soil, a chicken breast with the skin on or off. The USDA numbers are typical values from a sample, not a guarantee for the apple in your hand.

The usual reasons for differences

  • Variety and ripeness. The sugar in a fruit rises as it ripens, and different varieties differ widely.
  • Water. A food that has dried a little in the shop weighs less and has more nutrient per 100 g. Stored flour that has absorbed moisture is the opposite.
  • Cooking and preparation. Oil absorbed in frying, water absorbed when boiling, what is peeled away.
  • Which parts are counted. With or without skin, seeds, bones and trimmed fat.
  • Different energy factors. Databases and labels use slightly different rules for fibre and sugar alcohols, so energy can differ by a few per cent even when the nutrients agree.
  • Rounding. Labels round values; databases keep more digits. A label that says 0 g may mean less than half a gram.
  • Brand and recipe. A packaged food is a specific recipe and differs from the generic record.
  • Age of the data. The USDA SR Legacy database was last updated in April 2018, and some foods have changed since.

How much do they matter?

For everyday use, differences of a few per cent do not change anything. Larger differences usually come from comparing different states (raw against cooked, with skin against without) or different weights, not from error. When something looks very different, check those two things first.

A practical habit

Pick one source, use it consistently, and write down which version of a food you used (raw or cooked, with or without skin). That avoids most confusion. If you need medical precision, for a condition or a prescribed diet, follow the advice of your doctor or dietitian and the actual product label, not a general database.

Which is more accurate, a label or this database?

A label describes a specific product and is more relevant for it. A database describes a typical food.

Why does my app show a different value?

It probably uses a different record (cooked instead of raw, a brand instead of a generic food) or different rounding.
