Raw and cooked foods: why the numbers change

Cooking does not remove calories from lentils. It adds water, so each 100 g weighs less in energy.

The same lentils, two very different numbers

In the USDA data, raw whole lentils have 352 kcal per 100 g and 24.6 g of protein. The same lentils boiled without salt have 116 kcal and 9.0 g of protein per 100 g. Nothing was removed from the lentils in the pot. They soaked up water, going from 8.3 g of water per 100 g to 69.6 g per 100 g, so each 100 g of cooked lentils contains much less lentil than 100 g of dry ones.

The same thing happens with other staples in the data:

Food (per 100 g)Raw, kcalCooked, kcalWater raw, gWater cooked, g
Lentils3521168.369.6
Chickpeas3781647.760.2
Brown rice, long grain36712311.870.3

Cooked weight is roughly two to three times the raw weight for these foods, which fits: when something triples in weight by taking up water, its energy per 100 g falls to about a third.

Which to use

Use the value that matches the weight you have. If you weigh dry rice before cooking, use the raw figure for that weight. If you weigh the cooked rice on your plate, use the cooked figure. Mixing the two, for instance weighing cooked rice and applying the raw value, overstates the energy by about three times.

For recipes this means: calculate from the dry ingredients at the start (what goes in the pot), then divide by the number of portions. That is more reliable than weighing the finished dish, because cooking time and water change the final weight.

When cooking does change the nutrients

Besides water, cooking can change some nutrients: some vitamins such as vitamin C are partly lost into the cooking water or by heat, fat is added when you fry, and some foods lose fat when grilled. The USDA gives separate values for many cooked foods for this reason. Where the site has a cooked version of a food it is listed as its own page.

Cooked values from the pot

Spinach is an example of a food whose per 100 g values barely change: raw spinach has 23 kcal and 91.4 g of water per 100 g, and boiled spinach 23 kcal and 91.2 g. The leaves lose air and volume (a big pile becomes a small spoonful) and some water, but the proportion of water stays about the same, and so does the energy per 100 g. A portion of cooked spinach, though, weighs and contains far more leaf than the same weight of a raw portion by cup.

Why are cooked lentils so much lower in energy?

Because 100 g of cooked lentils contains about 30 g of dry lentils and 70 g of water.

Should I count raw or cooked?

Count the state in which you weigh the food.
