How much activity per week? What the guidelines say

What the official guidelines say about weekly amounts, with the MET-minute arithmetic.

What the guidelines say

The Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans, 2nd edition (US Department of Health and Human Services, 2018) say that for substantial health benefits adults should do at least 150 minutes (2 hours 30 minutes) to 300 minutes (5 hours) a week of moderate-intensity aerobic physical activity, or 75 minutes to 150 minutes a week of vigorous-intensity activity, or an equivalent combination. They also say that some physical activity is better than none, and that adults should move more and sit less. The Guidelines add that a simple rule of thumb is that 1 minute of vigorous-intensity activity counts the same as 2 minutes of moderate-intensity activity, and that both ranges are equivalent to about 500 to 1,000 MET-minutes a week.

Turning it into MET-minutes

Using the intensity classes from the same Guidelines (moderate 3.0 to 5.9 METs, vigorous 6.0 or more), the arithmetic is: 150 minutes x 3.3 METs is about 500 MET-minutes; 150 minutes x 6.7 METs is about 1,000. What this site does not say

It does not say how much activity *you* should do or whether a figure is right for you: the Guidelines are population guidance for healthy adults, and people with a health condition, pregnant women and children have separate advice. Ask a doctor or health worker before you change your activity level, especially if you have a condition or have been inactive. These pages are for information and estimates only.

Source: Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans, 2nd edition, US Department of Health and Human Services, 2018 (odphp.health.gov), read 13 November 2026; MET values: 2024 Adult Compendium of Physical Activities.

Do the guidelines say how many calories to burn?

No. They set minutes of activity by intensity, not kilocalories.

Is walking enough?

By the Guidelines' own classes, walking at 2.5 to 4 mph is moderate intensity (the guidelines list walking briskly as an example). The amount that is right for a person is for that person and their health worker to decide.

An estimate. MET values are averages for adults, not measurements of you. Treat every figure as a rough estimate. This site gives general information, not medical advice, and is independent of the Compendium authors and of the US government. See accuracy and limits.
