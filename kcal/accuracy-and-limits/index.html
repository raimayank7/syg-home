Accuracy and limits of MET calorie estimates

What the numbers can and cannot tell you, in the Compendium authors' own words.

What the authors say

The Compendium of Physical Activities site explains, on its "Corrected METs" page (read 13 November 2026), that the Compendium was developed in the late 1980s for use in epidemiologic and surveillance studies to standardise the MET intensities used in physical activity questionnaires, and that it was not developed to determine the precise energy cost of activities within an individual; it adds that the Compendium is frequently used outside its original scope. The page describes "standard METs", which assume a resting rate of 3.5 millilitres of oxygen per kilogram per minute for everyone, and notes the criticism that this value does not match every person's measured resting rate. The authors offer a separate method of "corrected METs" for adjusting for sex, body mass, height and age; this site does not use it and uses the standard METs as published, unchanged, as the Compendium asks.

What this means for the figures

  • The kilocalories shown are estimates for an average adult at your weight. Two people of the same weight can use quite different amounts.
  • A MET value covers a described activity at a described effort. "Running, 6 mph" in the table is a particular pace: your pace, the slope, the wind and the surface change the cost.
  • The figures include the energy you would use at rest, so they are total, not extra, energy.
  • A step count or a fitness watch uses different methods and may show different numbers.

What the figures are good for

Comparing activities with each other (a brisk walk against a run), seeing how time and body weight change the estimate, and understanding the minutes-and-MET-minutes language of the physical activity guidelines. They are not a prescription and not a guide to how much to eat. For advice about weight, food or exercise for your own health, ask a doctor, dietitian or health worker.

Source: Ainsworth BE et al., 2024 Adult Compendium of Physical Activities (Journal of Sport and Health Science, January 2024), pacompendium.com, the PDF "2024 Adult Compendium" read on 13 November 2026; unit conversions from the Compendium website (pacompendium.com/unite-conversions); intensity classes from the Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans, 2nd edition (US Department of Health and Human Services, 2018), read on 13 November 2026. The Compendium states that the Adult Compendium, Older Adult Compendium and Wheelchair Compendium are free to use for commercial purposes and asks users not to change MET values: none is changed here.

Should I use the corrected METs?

This site does not. If you want them, the Compendium site has a corrected MET calculator and spreadsheet examples.

Why does my watch show something different?

Watches estimate from heart rate, movement and your profile, with their own methods. They are also estimates.
